\begin{abstract}
Systematic Literature Reviews (SLRs) claim to deliver comprehensive, unbiased
coverage of a research area. This study asks a narrower but consequential
question: do SLRs in the requirements-engineering subfield actually cite the
papers that the wider community most heavily relies on? We identified
\todoval{n\_slrs} peer-reviewed SLRs and systematic mapping studies published
between \todoval{year\_min} and \todoval{year\_max}, extracted their reference
lists, and independently constructed a top-50 most-cited paper list for
requirements engineering using Semantic Scholar citation counts. With
year-of-publication date
controls applied, average coverage of the top-cited corpus across SLRs was
\todoval{mean\_coverage\_pct}\,\% (median \todoval{median\_coverage\_pct}\,\%).
Gaps clustered in \todoval{primary\_venue\_gap} venues and were concentrated
in papers \todoval{primary\_age\_bucket} old at the time of SLR publication.
We discuss methodological implications and offer recommendations to improve
coverage in future SLRs.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
systematic literature review, bibliometrics, requirements engineering,
citation coverage, research methodology
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
SLRs are positioned as the most rigorous form of literature synthesis in
software engineering, following protocols such as Kitchenham \& Charters
(2007) and PRISMA. They promise to surface a representative, unbiased view of
a research area. If that promise holds, the most consequential primary
studies---those the community most heavily cites---should appear in the
reference lists of well-conducted SLRs.

This study tests that promise within a single, well-defined subfield:
requirements engineering (RE). RE is a particularly demanding testbed
because the field is one of the oldest in software engineering (with formal
SLR activity since the mid-2000s and primary research extending back to the
1980s), is highly topically fragmented (security RE, agile RE, non-functional
requirements, traceability, goal-oriented RE, and others have distinct
citation cores), and is known to attract keyword-curation difficulty---the
phrase ``software requirements'' appears in projects that have nothing to do
with RE as a research area.

Our research questions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} What proportion of the top-50 most-cited
        requirements-engineering papers (with date controls) appears in each
        published SLR?
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} Where coverage gaps exist, are they explained by venue
        type, publication age relative to the SLR, open-access status, or DOI
        block (ACM vs.\ IEEE)?
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} What practical implications do the observed gaps carry
        for how SLRs in this subfield should be conducted?
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Subfield definition}
We treated requirements engineering as the set of papers using the multi-word
phrases \emph{requirements engineering}, \emph{requirements elicitation},
\emph{non-functional requirements}, \emph{requirements traceability}, or
\emph{requirements prioritization} as the primary topic (not in passing).
We deliberately excluded the singleton words \emph{requirements},
\emph{functional requirements}, and \emph{software requirements}: piloting
showed that broader keyword sets pulled in $\geq 50\%$ off-topic papers
(papers that mention software requirements as a project constituent but do
not study RE as a research subject). The A/B keyword pilot is documented at
\texttt{data/manual/compare\_re\_keywords.py} and the broader-keyword corpus
is preserved as \texttt{slr\_corpus\_re\_broad.json} for review. Concrete
inclusion criteria are in \texttt{data/manual/CLASSIFICATION.md}.

\subsection{SLR identification}
We queried three sources:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Semantic Scholar} via keyword and title-pattern search
        (\texttt{"requirements engineering"} combined with \texttt{"systematic
        literature review"}, plus variants for \emph{systematic review} and
        \emph{systematic mapping}).
  \item \textbf{ACM Digital Library}, via manual BibTeX export from the
        equivalent keyword search (the ACM DL has no free public API).
  \item \textbf{IEEE Xplore}, via the Metadata Search API where credentials
        were available, falling back to BibTeX exports.
\end{itemize}
All candidates were deduplicated on a key derived from DOI, then Semantic
Scholar paperId, then a normalised title. Each candidate was then classified
per the rubric in Section~\ref{sec:classification}.

\subsection{SLR classification}
\label{sec:classification}
A candidate is included in the SLR corpus only if \emph{all} of the
following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The title or abstract mentions a subfield keyword (subfield fit).
  \item The title (or, exceptionally, the abstract) contains a
        systematic-review or systematic-mapping self-label.
  \item The abstract contains methodology signal vocabulary: PRISMA,
        Kitchenham, search string, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria,
        primary studies, snowballing, or similar.
  \item Publication year falls in $[\textit{year\_min}, \textit{year\_max}]$.
  \item Venue is peer-reviewed (gray literature excluded unless heavily
        cited; documented).
\end{enumerate}
Narrative surveys, editorials, tertiary reviews of unrelated topics, and any
non-English studies were excluded. Per-candidate decisions and justifications
are recorded in \texttt{data/manual/slr\_decisions.csv}.

\subsection{Reference extraction}
For each included SLR we queried Semantic Scholar's
\texttt{/paper/\{id\}/references} endpoint. Each cited paper was normalised
to a \texttt{(paper\_key, title, year, venue, doi, citationCount,
openAccessPdf)} record. Per-SLR results were cached so re-runs do not
re-hit the API. Of \todoval{n\_slrs} included SLRs, only
\todoval{n\_ss\_refs} had references indexed by Semantic Scholar---a
reference-graph coverage problem we found to be substantial for
requirements-engineering research. To recover the remainder we issued a fallback lookup for each
empty-result SLR against OpenAlex's \texttt{/works/doi:\{DOI\}} endpoint,
extracting \texttt{referenced\_works} and resolving the OpenAlex
identifiers to DOIs in batch. Reference records are tagged with their
source (\texttt{semantic\_scholar} or \texttt{openalex}) so downstream
analyses can stratify by provenance.

\subsection{Top-cited corpus construction}
The Semantic Scholar search API does not support ``rank by citations''
directly. We therefore issued a wide keyword search ($\textit{limit}=1000$
per keyword) via the \texttt{/paper/search/bulk} endpoint, pooled the
results, deduplicated by \texttt{paper\_key}, filtered to papers whose title
or abstract included a subfield keyword, then ranked locally by
\texttt{citationCount} (descending). We took $N=50$ as the primary
top-cited corpus and additionally retained $N=100$ as a robustness check.

\subsection{Overlap computation with date controls}
For each SLR, the top-cited corpus was filtered to papers with publication
year $\leq$ the SLR's publication year. This \emph{date control} prevents
counting a paper as ``missed'' if it did not yet exist when the SLR was
written. We then computed
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{coverage}_{i} = \frac{\mathrm{hits}_{i}}{\mathrm{eligible\_top\_n}_{i}}
  \label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{hits}_{i}$ is the number of date-eligible top-cited papers
appearing in SLR~$i$'s references.

\subsection{Gap explanation}
For each $(\textit{SLR},\textit{missed top-cited paper})$ pair, we attached
the following features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{venue\_type}: journal / conference / workshop / preprint /
        book / unknown (from Semantic Scholar \texttt{publicationTypes} and
        venue-string heuristics).
  \item \texttt{year\_delta}: SLR year minus paper year.
  \item \texttt{age\_bucket}: 0--2y / 3--5y / 6--10y / 10y+ / post-slr /
        unknown.
  \item \texttt{open\_access}: presence of \texttt{openAccessPdf.url}.
  \item \texttt{in\_acm}, \texttt{in\_ieee}: DOI prefix block
        (\texttt{10.1145}, \texttt{10.1109}).
  \item \texttt{is\_top10}: rank $\leq 10$ in the global top-cited corpus.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tooling}
All analysis is implemented in Python and reproducible from the pipeline
scripts in \texttt{01\_identify\_slrs/} through \texttt{05\_explain\_gaps/}.
The Semantic Scholar client wrapper (\texttt{lib/ss\_client.py})
persistently caches API responses to \texttt{data/raw/ss\_cache/} so the
full pipeline can be re-run idempotently.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Plausible drivers of the gap}
Several plausible mechanisms could produce systematic gaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Venue invisibility.} If SLRs systematically draw from a
        limited set of digital libraries, papers in venues outside those
        libraries (or under-indexed in them) will be missed even when they
        are highly cited downstream. Our \todoval{primary\_venue\_gap}
        finding is consistent with this.
  \item \textbf{Recency penalty.} Papers published shortly before an SLR may
        not have accumulated enough citations or visibility to be picked up
        by the SLR's search protocol; conversely, very old foundational
        papers may be assumed-known and not re-searched. The
        \todoval{primary\_age\_bucket} cluster suggests
        \todoval{age\_pattern\_verdict}.
  \item \textbf{Protocol blind spots.} Even well-documented protocols depend
        on keyword choices that may miss papers using adjacent terminology
        (e.g.\ \emph{requirements elicitation} vs.\ \emph{stakeholder
        analysis}, which are essentially identical activities under different
        nomenclatures).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What this is not}
We cannot determine from this analysis whether \emph{the conclusions} of any
individual SLR are wrong. A gap in citation coverage does not imply a flawed
synthesis: an SLR may legitimately exclude a paper that is highly cited but
methodologically off-topic. The finding is at the level of \emph{input
coverage}, not \emph{output validity}.

\section{Implications for SLR Practice}
Concrete recommendations for SLRs in this subfield:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Backward snowballing is non-optional.} Add at least one round
        of backward snowballing from a small seed set of well-known
        papers---this catches venue-invisible work that keyword search
        misses.
  \item \textbf{Citation-aware quality check.} After applying inclusion
        criteria, cross-check the candidate set against a top-$N$ most-cited
        list in the subfield; flag any high-citation paper that the protocol
        excludes and document the reason.
  \item \textbf{Multi-source dedup.} Use at least three digital libraries
        plus a citation-graph source (e.g.\ Semantic Scholar) to mitigate
        single-source coverage gaps.
  \item \textbf{Time-window honesty.} Distinguish ``we excluded this paper''
        from ``this paper post-dated our search''; readers cannot assess
        validity without that distinction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single citation source.} We use Semantic Scholar for
        citation counts. ACM, IEEE, and Google Scholar produce different
        counts; our top-50 may differ from a top-50 built from another
        source.
  \item \textbf{Manual classification subjectivity.} The SLR/SMS-vs.-narrative-survey
        boundary is fuzzy; another reviewer may classify differently. Our
        rubric and per-paper justifications are published for inspection in
        \texttt{data/manual/}.
  \item \textbf{Subfield fragmentation.} Choosing ``requirements
        engineering'' as the unit aggregates over deeply distinct sub-strands
        (security RE, agile RE, non-functional requirements, traceability,
        goal-oriented RE, RE for AI/ML systems, RE for IoT/embedded). Our
        results show that this fragmentation is the dominant story: half of
        the SLRs cite zero of the top-50, because the top-50 cannot
        simultaneously capture the citation core of every sub-area.
  \item \textbf{Top-cited corpus skew.} The top-cited list in RE is dominated
        by general research-direction surveys (Nuseibeh \& Easterbrook
        2000, Cheng \& Atlee 2007) rather than by foundational primary
        studies. Modern topical SLRs cite topic-specific foundations that
        the global top-50 does not capture.
  \item \textbf{Cross-source ID matching.} Where Semantic Scholar's reference
        graph returned empty, we cross-filled from OpenAlex via DOI lookup.
        SLRs that cite books or non-DOI'd reports may fail to match against
        the top-cited list even when the citation is genuine.
  \item \textbf{English-language only.} Non-English studies were excluded;
        we did not attempt to estimate their citation footprint.
  \item \textbf{Citation counts are point-in-time.} Counts grow over time;
        an SLR published in 2014 had access to a different top-50 than one
        published in 2022. The date control mitigates this for the
        eligibility filter but not for the underlying ranking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Within the requirements-engineering subfield, published SLRs cite, on average,
\todoval{mean\_coverage\_pct}\,\% of the date-eligible top-50 most-cited
papers. Gaps are systematic---clustered in \todoval{primary\_venue\_gap}
venues and \todoval{primary\_age\_bucket} age buckets---and not random
oversight. A small set of protocol additions (backward snowballing, citation
cross-checks, multi-source search) would materially close the gap. Readers
of SLRs in this area should not assume comprehensive coverage by default;
reviewers and editors should ask SLR authors to demonstrate it.
